## The question

A language model can be steered at inference time by adding a direction to its
internal activations — a direction built from pairs of prompts that differ only
in the attribute you care about, such as whether a person is described as
disabled. Subtract that direction while the model generates, and the biased
behaviour is meant to fade, with no retraining. Several such methods exist, and
each one commits in advance to a shape for the bias it removes. Most assume the
bias lies along a single direction and can be cancelled with one vector.

The gap is that none of them checks whether that assumption fits the bias in
front of it. A method tuned for a bias that really does occupy one direction
gets applied, unchanged, to a bias spread across dozens. When it then
underperforms, the usual move is to search harder over layers and strengths
rather than to ask whether the assumed shape was wrong to begin with. This work
starts by measuring the shape.

## The approach

CAD is a diagnostic that runs before any steering. It collects activations on
contrastive prompt pairs, takes their differences, and applies a singular value
decomposition to that stack. The decomposition itself is close to prior
representation-reading work; the contribution is what the spectrum is used for.
Three numbers come out of it: how much of the variance the top direction alone
explains, how many directions are needed to cover most of the rest, and how that
rank shifts across the network's layers. Those numbers set the rank of the
projection and the layers to intervene at, and the projection is then applied at
one fixed strength with no search over it.

The design choice worth defending is the order. Existing methods fix the
intervention first and then tune it against the benchmark; CAD reads the
geometry and lets the geometry pick the intervention. If the top direction
explains most of the variance, a single vector is the right tool and the
diagnostic says so. If it does not, the same reasoning prescribes a projection
onto many directions instead, and the number of directions is read off the
spectrum rather than guessed. Rank and layers are decided on a held-out pool of
pairs before a single steered sentence is generated.

## What was found

The load-bearing result is that bias geometry is not constant. On a
housing-discrimination benchmark, the top direction of the contrastive
differences explains **67.9%** of their variance — the bias is nearly
one-dimensional. On a disability-medicalization benchmark, the top direction
explains only **10.2%**, and about forty directions are needed to cover most of
the signal. That difference, not the choice of algorithm, is what decides
whether one-direction steering works: it succeeds on the near-one-dimensional
housing bias, where single-vector methods already reach parity, and fails on the
forty-dimensional medicalization bias.

On the medicalization benchmark, held to a matched set of 250 evaluation items,
a closed-form rank-1 erasure lands at d = −0.03 — no effect. CAD's prescribed
rank-40 projection, run at its fixed strength, reaches **d = 0.337** on that
same 250-item set, statistically level with a strong rank-1 mean-difference
baseline (CAA, d = 0.341, overlapping 95% intervals) but without that baseline's
search over layer and strength. On a same-item paired comparison, the projection
also cost less in generation quality: scored by a separate language-model judge,
its drop was smaller than the baseline's by 0.35 points on a ten-point scale
(95% CI [0.18, 0.53]). Larger effect sizes were reachable only with additive
operators that traded quality away, up to d = 0.47 on the same benchmark at a
measurable cost to the text.

The rank and the layers above were prescribed before any steered generation. The
one quantity that came from tuning is the steering strength for those additive
operators, which fell as the bias grew more concentrated across four model
families — a descriptive trend, not a rule the clean projection depends on,
since that projection uses one strength throughout.

## Limitations

The effect is uneven. Broken out by disability category, two of the nine
categories on the medicalization benchmark — mobility, and genetic and
developmental conditions — do not reach significance after correction for
multiple comparisons. The headline fairness result is narrower than it first
sounds: on the housing benchmark, CAD raises approval for Black applicants from
55% to the same 100% rate as White applicants, but it does so by pushing both
groups to the ceiling. That equalises the rate; it does not demonstrate fair
allocation, and it rests on only twenty decisions. The relationship between
spectral concentration and steering strength also has a documented exception —
on one of the four model families, it inverts on the heritage-hallucination
benchmark, which the paper reports rather than sets aside.

Two caveats bound the claims further. The medicalization score is lexical: it
compares the odds of medical against professional vocabulary drawn from the
model itself, so it measures the surface form of the bias, and whether the
intervention removes non-lexical versions of the same harm is not measured. And
the method needs contrastive pairs that isolate the attribute, so it is
unsupervised only once those pairs exist.
