## The question

Four-bit quantization is what lets an eight-billion-parameter model run on a single consumer GPU, taking Llama-3.1-8B from roughly 16 GB to 5.6 GB. It also costs accuracy, and the standard remedy is mixed precision: hold a handful of layers at full precision and compress the rest. Which layers is the only decision.

GPTQ, OmniQuant and ResQ all rank layers by the activation-covariance Hessian trace — a per-layer scalar amounting to the mean squared norm of the inputs a layer receives — and protect the layers scoring highest. The premise is that numerical sensitivity stands in for functional importance: a layer fed large inputs is a layer whose errors matter.

That premise had not been tested against downstream behaviour, and a wrong ranking signal fails quietly. The model still runs, perplexity still looks reasonable, and the full-precision budget is spent on layers that were never carrying the computation, with nothing in the usual metrics to indicate it.

## The approach

The method treats quantization as a controlled perturbation rather than an error to be minimised — the move a lesion study makes in neuroscience: damage one component, observe which function breaks, and infer what that component was doing. Here the entire network is quantized to 4-bit NF4 first, leaving the model uniformly impaired. Full precision is then restored to exactly one sub-layer, and the measurement is how much task performance returns. Each of the 64 sub-layers of Llama-3.1-8B — 32 attention and 32 MLP — is measured this way on GSM8K, ARC-Challenge and MATH.

Recovery is conditioned on forward-flipped examples: items the full-precision model answers correctly and the 4-bit model gets wrong. Those are the items quantization demonstrably broke. The conditioning also makes rates comparable across benchmarks. Absolute accuracy differs considerably across the three, so a raw two-point delta means something different on each; as a fraction of the damage available to repair, the numbers sit on one scale.

What makes this diagnostic rather than optimisation is that nothing is tuned: no training, no search over configurations, and every measurement starts from a fresh copy of the quantized model, so restorations cannot compound or interact in an order-dependent way. The output is a map of which sub-layers carry which functions under compression. The mixed-precision model that follows is a consequence of reading that map, not the objective.

## What was found

The ranking signal is not weakly informative. Restoration rank and Hessian sensitivity rank correlate at **ρ = −0.506** across all 64 sub-layers of Llama-3.1-8B — inverted, not absent. Per task the correlation is −0.483 on GSM8K, −0.535 on ARC-Challenge and −0.255 on MATH. Weight-space SQNR, a different numerical criterion, correlates at +0.025 and cannot be distinguished from zero — the failure is not peculiar to the Hessian trace.

The second result is the more interesting one. Under Benjamini-Hochberg correction across 192 tests, no attention sub-layer reaches significance for ARC-Challenge and no MLP sub-layer for GSM8K. Attention layers 13 and 14 recover **48.4%** and **51.6%** of the arithmetic damage on their own, while contributing between 2.8% and 12.5% on the other two benchmarks; early MLP layers 6 and 7 recover across all three. The two families are dissociated by task rather than ordered on one axis of importance.

The dead zone was a prediction confirmed. Deep attention layers in Pre-LN transformers accumulate large activation norms while contributing little to the residual stream, which is the Curse of Depth; restoring them recovers exactly zero on ARC-Challenge across layers 15 to 31. The dissociation was an observation, not something the framework anticipated.

The pattern survives changes of model and quantizer. Mistral-7B gives ρ = −0.574 over its own 64 sub-layers. DeepSeek-R1-Distill reproduces the layer-13 attention result at 42.6% GSM8K recovery. Under GPTQ rather than NF4, layer-14 attention recovers 46.4% on GSM8K and 3.6% on ARC-Challenge.

Reading the map gives a deployable artefact: six of 32 sub-layers at full precision, statistically indistinguishable from the full-precision model on all three benchmarks (p > 0.05, Cohen's d ≤ 0.12), at **42%** of its memory and 106 tokens per second against 101 for the fully quantized baseline. Constrained to an identical six-layer budget, the Hessian-selected six recover **2.6%** of ARC-Challenge damage, indistinguishable from no intervention, while the probe-selected six recover most of it; the direct comparison gives p = 0.001.

## Limitations

Everything tested is a Pre-LN instruction-tuned transformer with RMSNorm. Whether the inversion holds for Post-LN or DeepNorm architectures is unknown. Base-model replication was attempted on OPT-6.7B and Pythia-6.9B and failed for a mundane reason: both score below chance on zero-shot ARC-Challenge, at 22% and 19%, which leaves nothing correct to flip and so nothing to measure. Log-likelihood scoring would be the way to reach them.

The recovery metric is bounded by how many examples actually flip — 31 on GSM8K, 36 on ARC-Challenge, 24 on MATH. MATH is underpowered at that count and no sub-layer survives correction there, so its results are inconclusive rather than negative.

The Hessian-guided baseline ranks layers by the activation-covariance trace alone. Deployed allocators do more — OBS-style updates, PCA decomposition, group-wise scaling, outlier handling — so the comparison isolates the bit-allocation signal and does not establish what any production system would select at the same budget.

The throughput figure is single-batch decoding on one GPU rather than a batched-serving measurement, and should not be read as a deployment benchmark.

Bootstrap intervals on ρ and on the per-task recovery rates are not yet computed.
