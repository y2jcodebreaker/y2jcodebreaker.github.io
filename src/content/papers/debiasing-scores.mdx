## The question

Ask a language model for tips on traveling alone, and it gives travel tips. Ask
the same question as a person with a developmental disorder, and the answer often
begins by telling you to consult your healthcare provider and pack a medical kit.
This is the medicalization of disability: treating the user's disability as a
medical matter when the question was not about health. The answer is polite and
helpful in tone, and it still treats the user as a patient.

Activation steering, which edits a model's internal activations while it
generates, is increasingly used against social bias, and whether it works is
usually decided by an automatic score. This work asks whether those scores can
see this kind of harm at all, and what a steering method looks like once it is
judged by a measure that can.

## The approach

The work runs in three steps, each motivated by the one before.

First, test the scores. Two kinds are common: counts of words from a lexicon,
here a word-list score from our own earlier version of this work, and the
sentiment and regard scorers (VADER and Regard) that the AccessEval benchmark
itself uses.

Second, measure the framing directly: a fixed rubric applied by three LLM judges
from three model families, an erasure check that asks whether the answer still
mentions the user's disability, and a separate judge of answer quality.

Third, design a steering method around what the measure shows. Existing methods
find their direction by contrasting inputs, the question with and without the
disability. That direction captures every way the two answers differ, including
the mention itself. Framing-contrast steering (FCS) contrasts outputs instead: the
model's own answers to disability questions, labelled by the judges as
medicalizing or clean. Both sides mention the disability and differ in how they
frame it. FCS removes that direction at two layers during generation with an
exact projection. There is no training and no tuning on the evaluation set.

Every experiment was pre-registered, with its outcomes named before the data
existed, including the ones that went against us.

## What was found

The scores fail. The word-list score is driven mostly by one word the model
echoes from the user's disability name. Word salad beats four of the five
steering methods on it, and inserting one unrelated sentence into every answer
beats all of them (d = 1.17, against 0.74 for the best method) while the judges
see slightly more medicalization. VADER barely tracks medicalization, and Regard
prefers word salad to real answers.

Measured directly, five steering methods (CAA, Angular Steering, FairSteer, SADI
and the CAD projection from our earlier version) either leave medicalization
unchanged or reduce it while removing 31 to 69 percent of the mentions of the
user's disability. A one-sentence prompt asking the model not to assume medical
needs is a strong baseline on Llama-3.1-8B.

FCS lowers judged medicalization on Llama-3.1-8B by 0.22 and 0.12 points on the
two primary judges, and by 0.15 on a third judge never used for fitting, while
keeping the disability, the answer quality and the perplexity. The reduction is
about the size of CAA's, without CAA's loss of quality or of disability mentions.
It beats directions fit on shuffled labels, adds to the explicit prompt, and
replicates on Mistral-7B, where the prompt alone does nothing.

## Limitations

The effect is modest: 10 of the 215 items that both primary judges could rate
move from medicalizing to clean on both, and on Llama the reduction is comparable to CAA's and the
explicit prompt's. The advantage of FCS is in what it does not cost. The study
steers two 7 to 8 billion parameter models on English questions from one
benchmark, with one generation seed per condition, and its evaluation set of 50
questions leaves the intervals wide.

The measure rests on LLM judges, two of which also label the data FCS is fit on.
A calibrated human study with two student raters found the judges stricter than
lay readers, and its human-judge correlations fell below the pre-registered bar,
so the judges are not claimed to be validated by people. Steering alone was not
shown to carry over to new questions, and on Mistral a shuffled-label direction
also reduced medicalization, though only while erasing the disability. The
critique covers our own word-list score and the use of AccessEval's metrics as a
measure of medicalization, not what those metrics were designed for.

## Earlier version

This page replaces an earlier preprint, *Contrastive Activation Decomposition*,
which argued that the spectrum of contrastive activation differences prescribes
the rank of a steering intervention, and which reported its results on the
word-list score above. Controls showed that the spectral statistic depends on how
the contrast set is built, and under the direct measure the CAD projection removes
the user's disability rather than the medical framing. Those claims are
withdrawn, and the earlier PDF is no longer posted.
